\small
Training diffusion transformers is usually presented as something that requires
industrial-scale compute. This resource teaches the opposite lesson through a real, runnable
pipeline arrived at over two months of empirically comparing image tokenizers, latent-space
parameterizations, and training hyperparameters, converging on a configuration that
pretrains a working text-to-image Diffusion Transformer (DiT) to sub-10 FID in 2.5 hours,
and fine-tunes it in 20 more minutes, on hardware any student can rent by the hour. Students
run this recipe themselves and evaluate their own results --- no pretrained weights are
distributed, so every checkpoint you get is your own.

  left=5pt, right=5pt, top=3pt, bottom=3pt]
\textbf{$\sim$2h50m total training on 4$\times$RTX~3090 (24GB) $\rightarrow$ sub-10 FID ---
about \$3--\$5 of rented compute for the whole pipeline.} \\
\footnotesize 2.5h pretrain (Exp.~1) + 20min fine-tune (Exp.~2). GPU pricing: Vast.ai
\$0.25/GPU/hr, RunPod \$0.46/GPU/hr (Aug 2026 quoted rates).

\vspace{-0.6em}
\paragraph{Learning objectives.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Explain latent-space diffusion and derive the flow-matching training objective
    (straight-line paths, velocity prediction).
  \item Contrast in-context (token-based) conditioning against AdaLN-Zero, and apply
    REPA-style auxiliary losses that align internal DiT features with a pretrained encoder.
  \item See how tokenizer/recipe choices, not scale, determine whether DiT training is
    affordable on consumer GPUs, and build a full data $\to$ precompute pipeline.
  \item Diagnose what a fine-tuning FID curve really measures, and score generations with
    learned human-preference models (PickScore, HPSv2) against FID/IS.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{-0.2em}
\paragraph{What students actually do.} The full notebook (13 sections) ties background
theory to the real training code, runs each model component individually (VAE tokenizer,
text encoder, REPA target, the DiT backbone), builds the data pipeline, runs pretraining and
fine-tuning, and evaluates with FID/IS and learned human-preference models. Every code cell
is real, executable code from the actual research codebase, not a simplified
reimplementation.

\vspace{0.5em}
\paragraph{Why does FID rise rather than fall?} Most likely a metric artifact, not
overfitting: both evaluation stages reuse the identical FID reference statistics, built once
from the 26{,}000-image pretraining photo set --- including for the SFT model, whose actual
training data (2{,}000 curated synthetic images) is a deliberately different distribution.
FID measures distance to whichever reference it's given, so specializing toward a new
distribution raises FID against the old one regardless of quality. The checkpoint scored in
Exp.~3 above (\texttt{ep-0000100}, the final and single worst-FID checkpoint) is still the
one PickScore/HPSv2 prefer 97.6\% of the time --- hard to square with ``the images got
worse,'' easy to square with a mismatched reference. Neither metric alone tells the complete
story here; students do not see these results before running the notebook, and reproduce and
reconcile all three themselves on their own trained checkpoints (Sections 10 and 12).
